\begin{abstract}
\noindent I introduce artificial intelligence into OG-UK, the UK calibration of the OG-Core overlapping-generations model, as an unanticipated shock announced in \genShockYear{}. AI lowers the labour share and raises output at given inputs over \genShockYear--\genHeadYear{}. Three scenarios are calibrated to UK sources (the IMF's 2026 UK Selected Issues paper and the OBR's productivity and fiscal-risks work), with the ``substantial'' US scenario of \citet{korinek2026} as an upper case. Gaps are measured against an OBR-central path that already includes the OBR's own AI productivity assumption. In the UK central scenario, with OG-UK's default capital closure ($\zetaK = \genZetaKCentral{}$), gross national income in \genHeadYear{} is \genUkCentralMainGniCentralAbs{} per cent \genUkCentralMainGniCentralDir{} that path and GDP \genUkCentralMainGdpCentralAbs{} per cent \genUkCentralMainGdpCentralDir{} it. The GNI gap is smaller because about three-quarters of the extra capital is foreign-owned and its return is paid abroad. Across the UK scenarios GNI is \genUkLowMainGniCentralShort{} to \genUkHighMainGniCentralShort{} per cent, and \genKorinekMainGniCentralShort{} per cent in the upper case. With private capital unable to flow in ($\zetaK = \genZetaKBound{}$), the UK central GNI gap is \genUkCentralMainGniBoundShort{} per cent. The real wage is below the path in every scenario in \genHeadYear{}, by \genUkCentralMainWageCentralAbs{} per cent in UK central, because the fall in the labour share outweighs the gain in output per hour. Aggregate consumption is also below the path in the UK scenarios while national income is above it, as households save out of a higher return. Two further arms bound the result from below: the same automation with technology unchanged, and an OBR-style stress test in which output at given inputs is set so that \genHeadYear{} GDP is unchanged. In the stress test UK central GNI is \genUkCentralStressGniCentralShort{} per cent. Debt/GDP is higher by \genDebtRuleYear{} in every arm, because the higher return reprices the whole stock of debt. The model's single income-tax schedule taxes labour about \genEffWedgePpCentral{} percentage points more heavily than capital, against the OBR's \genObrWedgeLo--\genObrWedgeHi{} points, so it understates the fall in revenue. With transfers adjusting so that households bear the fiscal cost until \genLastFlowYear{}, fewer than 1 per cent of adults alive in \genShockYear{} lose in the UK central main arm; in the stress test the average is about zero and \genCohStressDnCentralLosingPct{} per cent lose, including every group aged \genCohStressDnCentralAllLoseUpToAge{} or younger. In the main arm adults gain the equivalent of \genCohMainDnCentralAvgShort{} per cent of their remaining consumption and bequests on average, or \genCohMainDnCentralNoBeqAvgShort{} per cent of consumption if bequests are not valued, from a higher return on wealth they already hold. Two features of the model support the figure: a bequest is valued by the giver as well as funding the recipient's consumption, and from \genDebtRuleYear{} the fiscal cost falls on government consumption, which households do not value; without both, a first-order estimate puts the average at \genCohMainDnCentralChargedNoBeqAvgRange{} per cent with about half of adults losing. The bottom 25 per cent of lifetime earners gain more than the top 1 per cent, a property of the model's evenly spread wealth and equal per-head transfers rather than a finding about the UK.
\end{abstract}
